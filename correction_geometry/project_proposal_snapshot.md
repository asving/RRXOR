## 1. What is compositionality?

There is a generally shared intuition that the ability to compose outputs, behaviours or functions is a powerful technique to create more capable systems, and there has been a fair amount of work studying how compositional LLMs are at a behavorial level. However, there is a puzzle in applying this lens to the internal circuitry of a neural network. A deep network is, by definition, a composition of functions and so automatically compositional under our above definition.

However, there is a clear difference between different networks in how compositional they seem to our intuition (as we will see in examples below). I will argue that what we really want is that the network solves a task family by combining a few **computational units** (i.e., functions or subcircuits part of the computational graph of the trained network), each useful across many tasks — so that when a new task arrives, the expressivity *reachable in a short period of training* is much higher than raw parameter count suggests. This ties the decomposition of the circuit to the structure of the task, and therefore makes the decomposition not automatic. 

We define the **breadth** of such a circuit as the number of distinct computational units that feed into it and the **depth** as the number of computational units that it serially consumes before reaching the input. Examples will make clear how we are using these terms. We first explain a possible incentive for neural networks to learn compositionally broad circuits, coming directly from the number of distinct 'tasks' the network needs to solve.

## 2. Breadth: the pressure to share across tasks

The simplest setting is when each circuit is the solution to a particular task in our task family. For instance, in a next-token prediction task on a sequence generated by a HMM, the tasks might be indexed by the hidden state of the system (or some coarsening of such). The network then has to learn a different solution conditional on the hidden state, learn to estimate its belief distribution over these hidden states, and use this distribution to combine the different solutions. Let us first investigate the potential learning pressures in this simple compositional circuit.

An example to keep in mind throughout is the RRXOR sequence. The sequence is of the form ... {random-bit}{random-bit}{xor-of-previous-two-bits} ... One way to classify the tasks this way is as 'predicting a random bit' and 'predicting the xor of the previous two bits'. Which prediction takes precedence at the current context position is indexed by a belief distribution over which phase we currently are in ('the first random bit', 'the second random bit' or 'the xor bit').

Precisely: draw iid fair bits $r_1^{(k)}, r_2^{(k)}$ and form blocks $B^{(k)} = (r_1^{(k)},\, r_2^{(k)},\, r_1^{(k)} \oplus r_2^{(k)})$; concatenate the blocks and emit a window of length $L$ starting at a uniformly random offset $o \in \{0,1,2\}$. Each position $t$ then carries a hidden phase $p_t = (o+t) \bmod 3$: positions with $p_t \in \{0,1\}$ are free bits, and $x_t = x_{t-1} \oplus x_{t-2}$ whenever $p_t = 2$. The generator's minimal causal machine has 5 states (nothing pending / one pending bit $\times 2$ / pending xor $\times 2$). An observer ignorant of $o$ must synchronize: its belief state is the posterior over phase together with the last two bits, and exact enumeration of this filter gives 51 reachable states which minimize to exactly **36** under bisimulation (measured, `rrxor_routes.py`). The Bayes floor is $\ln 2$ at free positions and $0$ at xor positions once synchronized.

Each 'solution' receives positive gradient pressure on the tokens corresponding to its hidden states. On other hidden states, we might simplify the analysis by assuming that the gradient pressure is a random vector, and expect that the sum of the gradient contribution over all the other hidden states cancels out relative to the positive gradient pressure. 

From this perspective, any 'circuit' in the network receives a mean gradient relative to each task, and the total amplification any 'circuit' receives depends on what the total gradient vector across all tasks is. This will be large if there are many tasks with an 'aligned' mean-gradient vector, i.e., the way the circuit is used across different tasks is roughly identical. On the contrary, the total contribution will be ~zero if the circuit is used in a different way in each task.

To make this precise we need to fix what 'the gradient vector' means. For a task $\tau$ with data distribution $D_\tau$ and per-example loss $\ell_\tau(x;\theta)$, distinguish three objects:

$$\underbrace{\nabla_\theta \ell_\tau(x;\theta)}_{\text{per-example (random)}}, \qquad \underbrace{g_\tau(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_\tau}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \ell_\tau(x;\theta)\right]}_{\text{task gradient (the drift)}}, \qquad \underbrace{\hat g_\tau^{(B)}}_{\text{batch: drift} + \text{noise of covariance } \Sigma_\tau / B}.$$

In the HMM setting the tasks share one next-token loss and differ only in conditioning: $D_\tau$ is the distribution of contexts given hidden state $\tau$, so $g_\tau$ is the state-conditional mean gradient. For a circuit $C$, let $P_C$ denote projection onto $C$'s tangent directions; operationally, $P_C \nabla \ell$ is the gradient felt by a zero-initialized adapter inserted at $C$'s interface (section 5.2), which avoids having to attribute parameters to circuits. The alignment of two tasks on the circuit is

$$\mathrm{align}(\tau, \tau' \mid C) \;=\; \frac{\big\langle P_C\, g_\tau,\; P_C\, g_{\tau'} \big\rangle}{\big\|P_C\, g_\tau\big\|\;\big\|P_C\, g_{\tau'}\big\|},$$

and the total amplification per step is the aggregate drive $\big\|\sum_\tau w_\tau\, P_C\, g_\tau\big\|^2$, where $w_\tau$ is the fraction of loss-bearing tokens on which $\tau$ is active. With $n$ aligned consumers this grows like $n^2$; with unaligned (randomly oriented) consumers it grows like $n$, no better than noise; with sign-opposed consumers it can vanish at positive per-task norms. These are exactly the three regimes realized by the experiments below (B3, B1, B2 respectively).

## 3. Depth: the pressure to consume other units

We now explain a possible incentive for neural networks to learn compositionally deep circuits. At the same time, this also offers an explanation for why networks with a residual stream represent information linearly (explained to me by Francesco). The basic idea is that information that is consumed by many downstream circuits is best represented linearly - the attention and MLP operators add to the residual stream, but with a norm that is relatively small compared to the residual vector in the typical case. Therefore information represented linearly can persist for many layers in a relatively stable format.

The main obstacle for a transformer network is integrating information across many token positions, i.e., computing joint correlation functions in many variables. The difficulty rises with the number of variables because the *drift self-cancels*: for a genuinely joint function of $k$ inputs (a parity is the extreme case), flipping any one input flips the target, so per-example gradients cancel in matched pairs and the population gradient $g_\tau$ survives only through higher-order couplings in the weights. The per-example gradients are not small; they point in data-dependent directions that average away. We have measured this directly (section 5.1): across circuits of input degree 1/2/3 on the same architecture, learning time multiplies by $\sim 4$ per added variable while the gradient's drift-to-noise ratio drops in matching proportion, with $\tau \cdot \mathrm{SNR}$ constant to within a factor of 2. On tree-structured tasks the attenuation has a closed form: each latent level crossed multiplies the usable squared correlation by the channel's SDPI constant $\eta_\ell = \lambda_2(T_\ell)^2$, giving a geometric ladder of learning times (the dilution ladder, MODEL_LADDER.md). It is relatively easy for the network to compute a function that depends on the current token and the previous token, but geometrically harder for it to compute a function involving more positions.

However, when the token stream is such that some tasks are easy and depend on, say the current token and previous token, and in computing this easy solution, the network writes some information to an intermediate layer that can then be combined with a previous token to solve harder tasks, we will see precisely a compositionally deep circuit emerging.

RRXOR is the minimal case of this. The xor function is a two-position circuit, directly financed at every third position, and it forms almost immediately. Once it exists, its *violations* become a new intermediate variable that localizes the phase: the teaching signal for the phase circuit literally does not exist until the xor unit computes. We have now measured this handoff as a pair of curves (section 5.2): the demand for the phase code at the relevant interface is $\approx 0$ at initialization, is manufactured as the xor unit's supply forms, peaks during the corresponding loss drop, and decays to zero once satisfied. The same structure iterated at three nested scales is the matryoshka ($\mathbb{Z}_{27} \supset \mathbb{Z}_9 \supset \mathbb{Z}_3$) cascade of manufactured gradients, and the PCFG family generalizes it quantitatively: learning time per level multiplies by the inverse SDPI constant of the level crossed.

Two honest refinements from the falsifier record. First, depth-by-bootstrapping is about *speed and staging, never possibility* at our scales: six preregistered attempts to construct a task where sparse deep supervision cannot build the shallow scaffolds all failed (attention credit assignment builds tape-invisible, leap-hard intermediates from deep loss alone). Second, sharp stage boundaries require *gating* (the later gradient not existing until the earlier circuit forms, as in RRXOR/m27); mere complexity separation gives ordered but overlapping onsets.

## 4. Compositionality is a learning-dynamics notion

The upshot of sections 2 and 3 is that breadth and depth are selected by the same currency: coherent gradient drive. This makes compositionality a learning-dynamics notion rather than an expressivity notion. The expressivity that matters is the set of functions reachable in a short window of SGD from the current parameters; computational units are what make this set large, because aligned consumers accumulate drive on shared units, so new tasks inside the units' span are learned at composition cost rather than from-scratch cost.

The sharpest statement is on RRXOR: the full 36-state belief simplex and the modular xor-then-phase decomposition are both zero-loss solutions of the same task. The network takes the second, and we can now say why with numbers. Racing the routes with best-case direct supervision (same architecture and data budget): the xor rung reaches criterion in $\sim$175 steps; the 3-state phase circuit in $\sim$1250-1975; the full 36-way simplex in $\sim$1600-2625. So even the simplex's floor price exceeds the modular path's slowest part, and in the actual next-token task it is worse than slow: the loss only ever demands the decision-relevant quotient of the belief state, so the fiber beyond that quotient has $w \approx 0$ and is never financed (probes confirm it never forms in the deterministic task). Solutions are ordered by accessibility and financing, not by loss. Two corollaries we have verified: the unsupervised cascade pays the same per-stage prices as direct supervision (credit assignment is not the bottleneck for financed parts), and when several adequate circuits compete for one consumer, selection follows accessibility first, weight second, with incumbency contributing friction but not permanence (section 5.4).
## 5. Ease of learning: a measurable accessibility for every circuit

The preceding sections use 'gradient-accessible' qualitatively. This section makes it a number, per circuit, measurable at any point in training. (Derived jointly with the route-competition and demand/supply experiments of 2026-07-26/28; code in the RRXOR repo, correction_geometry/.)

### 5.1 The end-to-end version: drift over diffusion

SGD's update is a random vector. Decompose the per-example gradient as $\nabla_\theta \ell(x) = \bar g + \xi(x)$ with $\bar g = \mathbb{E}[\nabla \ell]$ the drift and $\mathbb{E}[\xi] = 0$ the diffusion. Over $T$ steps the drift accumulates coherently ($\sim T$) while the noise random-walks ($\sim \sqrt T$), so a circuit's parameters separate from noise after $T^* \sim \text{noise}/\text{drift}^2$. For a circuit $f$ define the per-example margin $m_f = \text{logit(correct)} - \text{logit(incorrect)}$ at $f$'s output slots, and

$$G_{\mathrm{disc}}(f \mid \theta) = \big\| \mathbb{E}_x\!\left[\nabla_\theta\, m_f\right] \big\|^2, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}(f) = \frac{G_{\mathrm{disc}}(f)}{\mathbb{E}_x \|\nabla_\theta m_f\|^2}.$$

The margin projection is essential and easy to get wrong: the raw CE gradient's drift is dominated by circuit-independent structure (measured: SNR $= 0.99$ identically across routes of very different difficulty, because "the answer lives in the answer alphabet" dwarfs the discrimination signal). Pilot result (1-layer transformer; routes of input degree 1/2/3 = lookup / xor / 3-parity): $\tau_{95} = 137 / 587 / 2250$ while $\tau \cdot \mathrm{SNR}$ stays constant to within a factor of 2 over the 16x range. So $\tau \propto 1/G_{\mathrm{disc}}$ with a shared constant: the within-architecture grading of circuit difficulty is the drift-to-diffusion ratio of its teaching signal. Classical notions (input degree, information exponent, multiplicative chain length) become statements about how $G_{\mathrm{disc}}$ scales, and the hard zeros of the information-exponent idealization appear as smooth attenuation at finite init scale and softmax temperature.

### 5.2 The per-part version: virtual adapters at interfaces

End-to-end $G_{\mathrm{disc}}$ grades whole input-output maps; modular circuits need the measurement relocated to each **interface**. At a site $k$ (layer, position class) where an intermediate variable $v_k$ should live, imagine a zero-initialized adapter $v_k \mathrel{+}= W\, \phi(h_k)$, with $h_k$ the part's input activations and $\phi$ its feature class. The real loss assigns the adapter a gradient every batch, $\nabla_W \ell = \delta_k \otimes \phi(h_k)$, where $\delta_k = \partial \ell / \partial v_k$ is the backpropagated error at the interface. Define

$$G_{\mathrm{disc}}(k \mid \theta) = \frac{\big\| \mathbb{E}_x\!\left[ \delta_k(x) \otimes \phi(h_k(x)) \right] \big\|^2}{\mathbb{E}_x \big\| \delta_k \otimes \phi(h_k) \big\|^2}.$$

The numerator is a cross-covariance between *demand* ($\delta_k$: what downstream coherently asks for) and *supply* ($\phi(h_k)$: what upstream currently offers). It vanishes in two distinguishable ways: consumer-gated ($\delta$ churns because downstream is unformed) and supplier-gated ($\phi$ lacks the correlate). The projection $P_C$ of section 2 is exactly this adapter construction, which is what lets us define per-task alignment on a circuit without attributing parameters to it.

Measured on m27 across 76 checkpoints (`m27_parts.py`), the manufactured-gradient cascade becomes a pair of curves: demand-for-$u$ (the xor value) is 0.30 at step 0 and collapses to 0.005 once its supply forms (probe $R^2 \to 0.99$): satisfied demand stops driving. Demand-for-phase9 is 0.0003 at init (gated), is manufactured as $u$'s supply rises (0.004 at step 600, 0.022 at 800), peaks at 0.036 during the exception-stage loss drop, and decays once satisfied. Three verbs, all now measured quantities: demand is **manufactured** by upstream formation, **destroyed** by satisfaction, and **decohered** by conflicting consumers (5.3).

### 5.3 Decoherence: how co-financing dies

The comp_icl transience re-read in this instrument (all 21 checkpoints of the $\varepsilon = 0.8$ trajectory): while held-out composition erodes from its 0.375 peak to $\sim$0.25, the cross-composition coherence of the binding-directed credit decays from a brief 0.156 peak to $\sim$0.05 with negative dips, and the credit's effective rank concentrates from 4.35 to $\sim$1.5. Per-mask demand persists; the *agreement* dies: $\|\sum_c w_c\, g_c\|^2$ shrinks at constant per-$c$ magnitudes. Transience is co-financing decoherence. [single seed so far]

### 5.4 Selection among adequate circuits

When two circuits can both explain one prediction, arbitration (route-competition grids plus a forced temporal-inversion crossover) follows a lawful order: accessibility dominates (a lookup route beats an xor route at preference 1.00 even at 8x weight disadvantage and from a positional disadvantage, surviving a complexity-reversal control); weight is a secondary, soft, quickly-saturating bias among accessibility-matched routes; incumbency contributes friction ($\sim$50x slower takeover) but not permanence: a slot owned outright by the harder circuit is still taken over once the easier route is enabled, falsifying arrival-order lock-in. Selection is ongoing competition under $G_{\mathrm{disc}}(\theta_t)$, history-dependent but not history-locked.
